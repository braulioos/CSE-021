A license plate is a string over the alphabet of 36 characters consisting of 26 uppercase latin letters and 10 numerals (0 through 9) (\emph{2 points for correct expression and 2 points for justification})

\begin{enumerate}


\item
(4 points)
How many 7-character license plates have at least 2 numerals? \\

\textbf{Answer:}

\[
36^7-\left(26^7+\binom{7}{1}(10)(26^6)\right)
\]

There are $36^7$ total 7-character license plates since each of the
7 positions has 36 choices. We subtract the plates with fewer than
2 numerals.

There are $26^7$ plates with no numerals. For plates with exactly
1 numeral, $\binom{7}{1}$ chooses its position, $10$ chooses the
numeral, and $26^6$ fills the remaining 6 positions with letters.
By the \textbf{Product Rule}, this gives

\[
\binom{7}{1}(10)(26^6).
\]

Therefore, by the \textbf{Complement Rule}, the number of plates with
at least 2 numerals is
\[
\boxed{36^7-\left(26^7+\binom{7}{1}(10)(26^6)\right)}.
\]

\item
(4 points)
How many 7-character license plates contain the word MILES as a consecutive substring?

\textbf{Answer:}

\[
3(36^2)
\]

The substring \texttt{MILES} has 5 characters, so it can start in
position 1, 2, or 3 of a 7-character license plate. Thus, there are
3 possible positions for \texttt{MILES}.

Once its position is chosen, the remaining 2 positions can each contain
any of the 36 possible characters. By the \textbf{Product Rule}, there
are
\[
36^2
\]
ways to fill these positions. Since there are 3 possible placements of
\texttt{MILES}, the total number of license plates is
\[
\boxed{3(36^2)}.
\]

\item
(4 points)
How many 7-character license plates consist of 3 different characters with 3 copies of one, 3 copies of another and a single copy of the last?
\emph{Ex: AAA3331, P33T3TT, ...}\\
\textbf{Answer:}

\[
\binom{36}{3}\cdot 3\cdot\frac{7!}{3!3!1!}
\]

First, $\binom{36}{3}$ chooses the 3 distinct characters that appear
on the plate. Of these 3 characters, there are 3 choices for the one
that appears only once, while the other two each appear 3 times.
For any such choice, the 7 characters can be arranged in

\[
\frac{7!}{3!3!1!}
\]

ways, where we divide by $3!$ for each set of 3 identical characters.
Therefore, by the \textbf{Product Rule}, the total number of license
plates is

\[
\boxed{\binom{36}{3}\cdot 3\cdot\frac{7!}{3!3!1!}}.
\]
\item
(4 points)
How many 7-character license plates have exactly 3 different numerals and 4 copies of the same letter?

\textbf{Answer:}

\[
\binom{10}{3}\binom{26}{1}\frac{7!}{4!}
\]

There are $\binom{10}{3}$ ways to choose the 3 different numerals and
$\binom{26}{1}$ ways to choose the letter that appears 4 times.
The resulting 7 characters can be arranged in

\[
\frac{7!}{4!}
\]

ways. We divide by $4!$ because the 4 copies of the letter are
identical. The 3 numerals are all different, so no further division
is needed.
Therefore, by the \textbf{Product Rule}, the total number of license
plates is

\[
\boxed{\binom{10}{3}\binom{26}{1}\frac{7!}{4!}}.
\]
\item
(4 points)
How many 7-character license plates are there that are all letters and they are in alphabetical order?

\textbf{Answer:} There are 26 letters in the alphabet, and we must choose 7 distinct letters.
Once the 7 letters are chosen, there is exactly one way to arrange them in
alphabetical order. Therefore, the total number of license plates is

\[
\boxed{\binom{26}{7}}.
\]

\end{enumerate}

An art museum with 12 different rooms just got a shipment of 9 new paintings (\emph{2 points for correct expression and 2 points for justification})
\begin{enumerate}
\item
(4 points)
How many ways can the museum curator arrange all 9 paintings among the 12 rooms in the museum?

\textbf{Answer:}

\[
12^9
\]

There are 9 distinct paintings, and each painting can be placed in any
of the 12 rooms. Since there is no restriction on how many paintings
can be in the same room, each painting independently has 12 choices.
Therefore, by the \textbf{Product Rule}, the total number of
arrangements is

\[
\boxed{12^9}.
\]

\item
(4 points)
How many ways can the museum curator arrange all 9 paintings among the 12 rooms in the museum so that each painting is in a different room?

\textbf{Answer:}

\[
{}_{12}P_9=\frac{12!}{3!}
\]

Since each of the 9 paintings must be in a different room, the first
painting has 12 choices, the second has 11 choices, and so on, down to
4 choices for the ninth painting.
Therefore, by the \textbf{Product Rule}, the total number of
arrangements is

\[
\boxed{12\cdot11\cdot10\cdots4
= {}_{12}P_9
= \frac{12!}{3!}}.
\]

\item
(4 points)
How many ways can the museum curator arrange all 9 paintings among the 12 rooms in the museum so that at least one painting is in the biggest room and exactly one painting is in the smallest room?

\textbf{Answer:}

\[
9(11^8-10^8)
\]

There are 9 choices for the painting placed in the smallest room.
The remaining 8 paintings can each be placed in any of the other
11 rooms, giving $11^8$ arrangements.

However, this includes arrangements where no painting is in the biggest
room. If the biggest room is also excluded, each of the 8 paintings has
10 choices, giving $10^8$ such arrangements.

Therefore, by the \textbf{Complement Rule} and \textbf{Product Rule},
the total number of arrangements is

\[
\boxed{9(11^8-10^8)}.
\]

\item
(4 points)
Paintings A,B, and C are all painted by the same artist. How many ways can the museum curator arrange all 9 paintings among the 12 rooms such that paintings A,B, and C are all in the same room?

\textbf{Answer:}

\[
12^7
\]

There are 12 choices for the room containing A, B, and C. Each of the
remaining 6 paintings can independently be placed in any of the 12
rooms, giving $12^6$ possibilities.

Therefore, by the \textbf{Product Rule}, the total number of
arrangements is

\[
\boxed{12\cdot12^6=12^7}.
\]
\item
(4 points)
How many ways can the museum curator arrange all 9 paintings in 3 of the 12 rooms such that each room has exactly 3 paintings? 

\textbf{Answer:}

\[
\binom{12}{3}\binom{9}{3}\binom{6}{3}
\]

First choose 3 of the 12 rooms, which can be done in
$\binom{12}{3}$ ways. After labeling these three rooms, choose 3 of
the 9 paintings for the first room, giving $\binom{9}{3}$ choices.
Then choose 3 of the remaining 6 paintings for the second room, giving
$\binom{6}{3}$ choices. The remaining 3 paintings must go in the
third room.

Therefore, by the \textbf{Product Rule}, the total number of
arrangements is

\[
\boxed{\binom{12}{3}\binom{9}{3}\binom{6}{3}}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
